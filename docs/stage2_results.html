Reading the curves

The shape is the published one. Flat, then a knee, then collapse — matching the DeltaNet paper's MQAR result. That was the precondition for trusting anything downstream, and it holds.

The knee lands where the theory says it should. With four heads over a 64-wide model, each head's state is 16×16, so rank(S) ≤ 16. Both delta variants hold ≥95% through exactly 16 pairs and break immediately after. A capacity that matches head_dim to the integer is the rank ceiling asserting itself, not a coincidence of tuning.

The decay gate behaves as an evictor, not an expander. Gated DeltaNet beats DeltaNet only at 32 pairs, where the state is genuinely contended — worth +9.6 points. It changes nothing at 4, 8, or 64, and does not move effective capacity. That is the predicted signature: decay frees slots from stale patterns; it cannot raise min(d_k, d_v).

These are single-seed runs with no error bars. The gaps at 4, 8, and 16 pairs — where Gated DeltaNet trails DeltaNet by 0.2 to 1.3 points — are almost certainly seed noise and should not be read as a real ordering. Only the 9.6-point gap at 32 pairs is large enough to be suggestive, and even that is one seed.

Before these become the reference every later stage is compared against, they need replication across seeds. Otherwise a Stage 4 mechanism that appears to beat the baseline by a few points may just be beating noise.

What has to happen next

Beating 75% at 32 pairs is incremental. Moving effective capacity from 16 to 32 is the real target, and the plan gives exactly two levers for it — raise the ceiling, or lower the demand on it.

Raise it: kernel feature maps project keys into d_φ > d_k, changing the ceiling to min(d_φ, d_v) directly, at a compute cost that scales with d_φ. Lower the demand: cross-key deduplication refuses to spend a slot on a value the state already holds — which is why the redundancy axis r exists, and why these r = 0 curves are only the first row of the grid. The full sweep crosses all five redundancy levels.
